# Argument Playbook — Building The Persuasive Case

How to turn project facts into an argument a case officer can approve. Read at Phase 3, before drafting, and whenever a section feels weak.

## 1. Write for the delegated report

Most householder applications are decided by a single officer under delegated powers. That officer must produce a report justifying their recommendation against policy. The statement's job is to *pre-write that report's reasoning* from the applicant's side: identify every material consideration the officer must address, and hand them an evidenced, policy-anchored conclusion for each. When the statement has already done the officer's analytical work — constraints checked, policies cited, harms assessed and mitigated — approval becomes the low-effort, low-risk option.

The corollary: never leave the officer an unanswered question. An unaddressed issue is homework; homework invites a defensive refusal or a request for more information.

## 2. The authority hierarchy

Anchor every argument to the strongest authority available. Codified beats discretionary; discretionary beats subjective. Descending order of weight:

1. **Statute** — above all, section 38(6) of the Planning and Compulsory Purchase Act 2004: applications are determined *in accordance with the development plan unless material considerations indicate otherwise*. This is why development-plan-policy compliance is the spine of the case: demonstrate compliance and the statutory default is approval.
2. **Established case-law doctrines** — cite doctrines, not half-remembered case names: the fallback position, the requirement that considerations be material, consistency in decision-making. If a specific case is to be named it must be verified this job, never recalled from memory.
3. **The development plan** — the adopted local plan / core strategy policies, cited by number and name from the downloaded documents. These are the officer's own binding tests; showing compliance with each relevant policy in the officer's own vocabulary is the single most persuasive move available.
4. **The NPPF** — national policy; cite paragraph numbers only from the copy downloaded this job (numbering changes between revisions). Especially useful where national policy is warmer to the proposal than local wording, and for framing (good design, effective use of land, presumption in favour of sustainable development).
5. **SPDs and design guides** — the LPA's householder design guide sets the measurable tests officers actually apply (separation distances, 45-degree rules, dormer guidance). Quote their numbers and show the scheme passing each test.
6. **Appeal decisions and local precedent** — nearby approvals for comparable development, or characteristics already present in the street scene. Persuasive, not binding: "establishes a very clear and robust precedent".
7. **Site-specific evidence** — measurements, photographs, sightline studies. Powerful because verifiable, but deploy in service of a policy test rather than free-floating.
8. **Subjective design judgement** — the weakest tier. Never let an argument rest solely here; aesthetic claims must ride on context evidence ("echo the scale roof pitch and visual language of the original eaves gablets") or policy language.

An argument sitting purely on tiers 7–8 is a vulnerability. Lift it up the hierarchy or flag it to the user as a risk.

## 3. Argue only material considerations

Officers may only weigh material planning considerations. Spend words exclusively on them: character and appearance of the street scene, residential amenity (overlooking, overshadowing, overbearing impact, noise), heritage assets and their setting, highway safety and parking, flood risk and drainage, trees and landscaping, ecology, accessibility, use and intensity.

Never argue from non-material grounds — they waste credibility: property values, private boundary or covenant disputes, loss of a private view, the applicant's personal finances (need may be described as *accommodation need*, never affordability pleading), competition, or who the neighbours are.

## 4. The pattern library

Each pattern below names the move, shows it in the gold standard, and explains why it works on the institutional reader.

### P1 — Human need framed as planning merit

State the household's real story early and connect it to a recognised societal pressure so private need reads as public interest. *"The fundamental driver for this application is the evolving needs of the applicants family. This directly reflects the growing necessity for multi generational living within the UK amidst the current housing crisis."* Need is demonstrated with specifics (ageing parents moving in, a son's sixth form and university education) — never pleaded. This gives a sympathetic officer the "planning balance" material they need to weigh in favour.

### P2 — The proactive constraint sweep

Prove the negatives before anyone asks. Dedicate section 3.0 to constraints checked and cleared, with sources and figures: *"Public records indicate that the nearest listed structure is Hall Farmhouse which is a Grade II listed building situated approximately 392 metres away."* Each cleared constraint (conservation area, flood zone, TPOs) is stated with evidence and closed with a firm conclusion (*"absolutely no adverse impact"*). This removes the officer's homework and signals a diligent, trustworthy applicant team.

### P3 — Self-imposed restraint

Present every design limitation as the applicant's conscious, principled choice — often the more expensive one: *"the applicants consciously imposed a strict design constraint upon themselves to protect the existing garden space"*; *"While this is a more financially intensive structural solution this approach successfully mimics a traditional pitched roof."* Restraint reframes the scheme from "how much they are taking" to "how much they gave up", which defuses the instinct to negotiate it smaller.

### P4 — Alternatives considered and discounted

Show the optioneering: name the rejected alternative and the amenity-based reason. *"Early design explorations considered a ground floor extension… This approach was ultimately discarded to prevent further sprawling development across the plot."* / *"A traditional apex roof was discounted as it would raise the ridge to an unacceptable height."* Officers must consider whether harm could be avoided by an alternative; answering that question pre-emptively closes the "why not something smaller?" line of refusal.

### P5 — Precedent and context evidence

Root every design choice in what already exists nearby: the neighbouring taller ridge (*"establishes a very clear and robust precedent for a higher permissible ridge line"*), estate-wide motifs (gablets, cottage bar glazing, segmented brick arches). Character-based objections collapse when the feature is demonstrably part of the established character. Use annotated photography and name the comparator properties.

### P6 — Betterment

Where the site has an existing defect, make fixing it a headline benefit: *"this upward extension presents a valuable opportunity to rectify and significantly enhance the architectural quality of the rear elevation."* The scheme then offers positive planning gain, not merely an absence of harm — and refusal means keeping the defect. Criticise the defect architecturally (*"poorly composed and visually unresolved"*), never any person.

### P7 — Quantified verifiable specifics

Numbers and named products beat adjectives: 392 metres, sill heights exceeding 1.7 metres, ten degrees steeper, Velux PK08, fink style trusses. Specificity signals competence, survives checking, and converts a subjective claim into a factual one. Every number must trace to user input or logged research.

### P8 — Pre-offered conditions and concessions

Volunteer the mitigation before it is demanded: *"the applicant is fully agreeable to fitting the fixed lower pane with obscure glass should the planning officer deem it necessary."* This hands the officer a ready-made planning condition — a path to approve-with-conditions instead of refuse — and signals a cooperative applicant. Deploy on any element where a residual doubt could linger.

### P9 — Calibrated absolutes

Absolutes (*"absolutely no"*, *"zero"*, *"entirely"*) only where evidence is airtight; honest hedges (*"likely serving a non habitable bathroom space"*) where it is not. Full calibration guidance in the style guide §5. One caught overclaim taints every other claim in the document.

### P10 — The respectful close

The conclusion recaps need, absence of harm, and policy compliance, then asks for the decision in institutional language: *"we respectfully submit that the scheme represents considerate and well thought out development. We therefore formally request that planning permission be granted without delay."* Confidence without presumption; the request is always made explicitly.

### P11 — The permitted development fallback

Where parts of the scheme (or a less sympathetic alternative) could be built without permission under the GPDO, the fallback is a material consideration: the realistic prospect of a worse PD outcome weighs in favour of approving the better designed application. Deploy only after verifying PD rights actually apply (no Article 4 direction, rights not removed by condition, limits genuinely met) — an incorrect fallback claim is an own goal. Frame as: the applicant could pursue X as permitted development yet has chosen to seek permission for a more considered scheme.

## 5. Assembling the case

For each element of the scheme, run: **fact → material consideration → policy anchor → pattern**. Example from the exemplar: crown roof (fact) → character and street scene (consideration) → local design policy and NPPF design chapter (anchor) → P3 restraint + P4 alternatives + P5 precedent (patterns) → bespoke section 7.0.

Sequence arguments so each section deposits evidence the later sections withdraw: context (2.0) establishes the estate's motifs; appearance (5.0) shows the scheme using them; the conclusion collects everything as "overwhelming contextual evidence". Cross-reference figures at the exact sentence where the visual proof is needed.

## 6. Handling genuine weaknesses

Where the scheme has a real vulnerability, do not hide it — officers find it, and concealment converts a manageable issue into a credibility collapse. Instead: acknowledge the issue in its own bespoke section (§6 of structure guide), evidence why the impact is acceptable or mitigated, offer a condition (P8), and show the restraint already exercised (P3). If a weakness cannot be honestly mitigated, tell the user plainly before drafting around it — they may amend the design, and that conversation is worth more than any wording.
