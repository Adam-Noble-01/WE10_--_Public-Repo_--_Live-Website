# Style Guide — Voice, Punctuation, Lexicon

This guide defines the house prose. It is derived line-by-line from the approved gold-standard statement (`assets/exemplar--gold-standard.md`), which received strong feedback from both borough and parish councils. Deviating from it produces a document that reads as someone else's work.

## 1. Register and stance

Write as a confident professional advocate addressing an institution. The voice is courteous, assured, and relentless — never pleading, never aggressive, never casual. Three registers blend throughout:

- **The advocate** — argues the case: "The comprehensive evidence and high quality documentation submitted within this application present an undeniably robust and common sense case for approval."
- **The architect** — demonstrates design literacy: "the proposed pitched roof dormers have been specifically designed to echo the scale roof pitch and visual language of the original eaves gablets."
- **The courteous professional** — respects the process: "We respectfully submit that the scheme represents considerate and well thought out development. We therefore formally request that planning permission be granted without delay."

Address the reader's institution, never the reader personally. The officer is "the planning officer" or "the local planning authority" and is always treated as reasonable and diligent.

## 2. Person and voice

- The practice speaks as **"we"** and by name: "We have specifically designed a study nook…", "Noble Architecture has identified the probable need…". Never "I".
- The client is **"the applicant"/"the applicants"** in the third person, with family members named by role: "the applicants family", "the ageing parents", "their son". Use the applicant's name sparingly and formally ("Dr Nagendra and his family have a demonstrable need…").
- The building is **"the host dwelling"**, **"the property"**, or **"the application site"**. The design is **"the proposal"**, **"the scheme"**, or **"the proposed development"**.
- Prefer active constructions that credit deliberate design: "the applicant consciously opted to cap the proposed ridge height" rather than "the ridge height was capped".

## 3. The comma-light rule (main statement only)

The main statement (sections 1.0 through the conclusion) is written in a deliberate comma-light rhythm. Sentences flow as unbroken declarative units. This is a signature of the house style — replicate it faithfully.

**How to write comma-free prose that stays readable:**

- Keep sentences to one idea. If a sentence needs a comma to survive, split it into two sentences instead.
- Open sentences with connectives and run straight on without a comma: "Furthermore to alleviate general domestic clutter one of the existing bedrooms will be repurposed as a dedicated store room." / "Consequently they actively discounted a new ground floor extension in favour of a more financially intensive upward expansion approach."
- Let short lists run together without commas where the reading survives it: "without causing any detrimental harm to the residential amenity privacy or sightlines of any neighbouring occupiers". If a comma-free list collapses into ambiguity, restructure the sentence rather than reaching for commas.
- Sentence rhythm: mostly 15–30 words, punctuated by short emphatic sentences for landing blows: "This ensures the addition reads as a cohesive part of the building."

**Where commas remain correct:**

- The **Supplementary Drawing Notes** section uses conventional British punctuation throughout — notes migrate onto drawings and are read as standalone technical text: "The current roof structure utilises restrictive fink-style trusses, meaning a complete roof replacement is fundamentally required."
- **Table cells** use conventional punctuation: "Facing brickwork to strictly match existing in colour, size, tone, texture, and mortar colour."
- Figure captions, header block, and boilerplate general notes: conventional punctuation.

## 4. Connective openers

Deploy these sentence-openers to chain reasoning (no comma after them in the main statement): Furthermore / Consequently / Crucially / Ultimately / Simultaneously / Additionally / Alongside this / Despite this / Given this / Therefore / However. Vary them — never open consecutive sentences with the same connective.

Emphasis frames for pivotal points: "It is vital to emphasise that…", "Crucially this upward extension presents a valuable opportunity to…", "The fundamental driver for this application is…".

## 5. The intensifier lexicon — and its calibration

Intensifiers are the engine of the persuasive register, but their power comes from disciplined deployment.

**House vocabulary:** meticulously, rigorously, thoughtfully, carefully, consciously, deliberately, specifically, strategically, sensitively, sympathetically, seamlessly, entirely, absolutely, strictly, firmly, fundamentally, demonstrable, undeniable, comprehensive, granular, robust, paramount, considerable, substantial, exceptionally, highly, overwhelming.

**Quality descriptors:** high quality, well integrated, carefully proportioned, subservient, cohesive, harmonious, sympathetic, considerate, well thought out.

**Negative descriptors** (reserved for what the scheme fixes or avoids): visually intrusive, unsympathetic, poorly composed, visually unresolved, discordant, bulky, austere, drab, ad hoc, overbearing, sprawling.

**Calibration — the honesty gradient.** Absolutes are spent only where the evidence is airtight; measured language is used where it is not. The exemplar claims "absolutely no adverse impact" on heritage because the nearest listed building is 392 metres away — verifiable and unanswerable. But it hedges honestly where the fact is unverified: "likely serving a non habitable bathroom space". An absolute attached to a checkable falsehood hands the officer a reason to distrust the whole document. Match claim strength to evidence strength, always.

## 6. Headings

Capitalise Every Word In Headings Including Short Words: "Project Drivers And Accommodation Needs", "Justification For First Floor Expansion", "Protection To Neighbours Privacy", "Note To Reader". This applies to section titles, subsection titles, drawing-note titles, and table headers. (Heading markup grammar is in `structure-and-formatting.md`.)

## 7. Spelling, dates, numbers

- British spellings exclusively: -ise verbs (utilise, prioritise, minimise), colour, metre, storey/storeys, neighbouring, programme, artefact, grey.
- Dates in the header block: `30ᵗʰ March 2026` — day with superscript ordinal (ᵗʰ ˢᵗ ⁿᵈ ʳᵈ), month name, full year. Revisions lettered: "Revision A", "Revision B".
- Measurements metric: metres, millimetres. Write distances as digits with the unit ("approximately 392 metres", "exceeding 1.7 metres"). Small counts in prose as words ("four panel folding door system", "ten degrees steeper").
- Specifics beat generalities: name product types and codes where known ("Velux size code PK08", "EPDM or GRP system", "fink style trusses"). Precision signals competence and verifiability.

## 8. UK planning lexicon

Use the British planning system's own vocabulary — the officer thinks in these terms:

householder application · local planning authority · planning permission · determination · material planning considerations · development plan · curtilage · plot · dwellinghouse · C3 use class · street scene · host dwelling · principal elevation · subservient · vernacular · amenity · residential amenity · privacy amenity · overlooking · obscure glazing · sill height · separation distance · ridge line / ridge height · eaves · gablet · dormer · crown roof · rooflight · soldier course · segmented brick arch · permitted development · Article 4 direction · Conservation Area · Listed Building · Tree Preservation Order · Flood Zone 1/2/3 · green belt · Building Regulations · delegated report · planning condition · section 106 · supplementary planning document (SPD) · National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF)

## 9. Americanism blacklist — binding

These words mark a document as machine-written or foreign to the UK system. Never use them:

| Never | Always |
| :--- | :--- |
| zoning, rezoning | planning policy, allocation, use class |
| permit, permitting | planning permission, consent |
| setback | separation distance, set back from (verb only) |
| lot | plot, site, curtilage |
| yard / backyard / front yard | garden, rear garden, frontage |
| sidewalk | footway, pavement |
| curb / curb appeal | kerb / street presence, kerb appeal |
| story / stories | storey / storeys |
| first floor (meaning ground level) | ground floor (UK first floor = the floor above) |
| neighborhood | local area, surrounding area, estate |
| building code | Building Regulations |
| remodel | refurbish, alter, remodel is banned |
| siding | cladding |
| ADU / in-law suite | annexe, ancillary accommodation |
| city hall / county | council offices / borough, district, county council (correct UK body) |
| -ize spellings | -ise spellings |

## 10. Never do

- Bullet points or numbered lists in the main statement — it is continuous prose, tables, and figures only. (Drawing notes are also prose paragraphs under coded headings.)
- Emotive begging ("we really hope", "please consider") — need is *demonstrated*, never pleaded.
- Attacking neighbours, the council, or previous decisions. Existing eyesores are criticised architecturally ("poorly composed", "visually unresolved") and never attributed to a person.
- Uncalibrated absolutes, invented specifics, or policy citations from memory.
- Empty superlatives without a fact attached. Every "rigorously" must sit next to the thing that was done rigorously.
