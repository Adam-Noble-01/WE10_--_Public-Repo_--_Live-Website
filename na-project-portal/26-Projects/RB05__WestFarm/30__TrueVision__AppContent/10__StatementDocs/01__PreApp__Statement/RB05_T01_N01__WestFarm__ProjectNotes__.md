### INTRODUCTION

The dwelling we have designed is supposed to be a highly convincing, sympathetic, true-to-form, faithful Georgian home. We've gone through several design iterations and sessions of adding and removing ornamentation until we got to the point where we felt it was right. The kind of vibe we're trying to achieve is a Georgian farmhouse, like a Georgian country estate.

Rather than extending this over an outrageous amount of the floor area (which would make it feel more like a stately home rather than a large agricultural manor, and what likely could have been the seat of parish and manor lordship type farmhouse, rather than it feeling like an aristocrat's house), we achieved this by designing what we've called the coach house. This is a building designed to look like an old stable that's been converted, and this will house guest accommodation and utility room spaces utilised by the occupants of the main house, while keeping everything separate, away from everyday family living in a self-contained space.

The coach house is attached by the doorway that comes through into the east atrium, which is the colonnade entrance, the entrance that has the flat roof and the columns that lead through into the kitchen. The kitchen itself is supposed to appear like a converted barn that's been tastefully and faithfully converted. Internally, there is exposed timber work again, echoing that feeling of a British agricultural building that's been converted, and then this links back to the house.

With an infill structure in the style of Vale Garden Houses, David Salisbury, or any other company at that level that makes superb, high-quality British classical orangeries, it filled a nice linking space. We did originally try a fully glazed, more modern winter garden type thing, but it detracted and didn't truly link the spaces in a way that felt true to the character and charm of an actual period Georgian building. 

The building's exterior is constructed from mostly rough stonework, with certain details using cut stone, such as the cut stone corner coins, the cut stone columns, and pilasters. All of the columns and pilasters used are in the Tuscan order and, again, faithful to the Georgian design style, which borrows heavily from Greco form and classic proportions, etc. All windows have been ratioed and proportioned to be as faithful as possible to those design principles.

## EXISTING DWELLING 

### Existing Architectural Character

The existing dwelling is a highly individual late twentieth-century house whose present appearance reflects a number of successive alterations and additions rather than a single coherent architectural composition. The original property dates from the 1960s, with later interventions including the large glazed conservatory and substantial thermal upgrading works. The overall character is predominantly low-rise and horizontal, combining a long bungalow-like principal range with a more substantial one-and-a-half-storey element at one end. The result is architecturally idiosyncratic and strongly reflective of the period and personal priorities of its former owner, rather than of an established local vernacular or clearly identifiable architectural style. 



![23__BNT250373_31](./02_StatementDocs__Content__Images/01__ExistingSite/23__BNT250373_31.jpg)

Fig #.# |  Existing Dwelling  -  A Large sprawling footprint

### Form and Massing

The principal form is broad, low and spreading, with extensive roof planes extending across an irregular footprint. A large pitched roof dominates much of the dwelling, with secondary gabled elements, the conservatory and later service structures creating a somewhat additive composition. The building consequently reads less as a deliberately ordered country house and more as a substantial dwelling that has evolved incrementally in response to changing functional requirements. Despite its considerable floorspace, its massing remains comparatively low, with much of the accommodation arranged at ground-floor level.

![](./02_StatementDocs__Content__Images/01__ExistingSite/28__BNT250373_35.jpg)

Fig #.# |  Existing Dwelling  -  A Birdseye View Of Rear



### Relationship with the Site

The existing dwelling occupies a relatively central but visually secluded position within the wider holding, set well back from the public highway and surrounded by extensive mature landscaping, woodland, lawns and water features. Its low-rise, predominantly horizontal form allows it to sit below the surrounding tree canopy and remain heavily screened from wider views, with the principal developed area concentrated within an established landscaped core rather than projecting into the more open countryside beyond. The house is approached through a sequence of private drives and landscaped spaces, with the lake, helicopter landing area, ancillary buildings and mature planting creating a loose estate composition around it. Although the setting is exceptionally attractive, the current dwelling does not fully exploit it architecturally, with relatively limited visual and spatial connection to the wider landscape when compared with the opportunities presented by the site.

![01_10__ExistingSite__AerialShot-01__](./02_StatementDocs__Content__Images/10__ExistingHouse__Photos/01_10__ExistingSite__AerialShot-01__.jpeg)

### Existing Accommodation

The dwelling provides approximately 456 m² of residential accommodation across the two principal floors based upon the available floor plans. It contains four bedrooms, three of which are situated at ground-floor level, together with three principal reception rooms comprising the lounge, dining room and conservatory. The first floor contains the fourth bedroom alongside a substantial snooker room and approximately 8.3 m x 8.0 m loft area. The sales particulars similarly describe the property as a four-bedroom dwelling with three reception rooms and three bathroom/shower facilities. 

### Internal Planning and Function

Internally, the dwelling displays an unconventional arrangement that appears to have developed around the particular lifestyle and technical requirements of its former owner. The principal living room is extremely large, while the kitchen is comparatively modest and separated from the principal entertaining accommodation. Bedrooms, bathrooms, utility accommodation and circulation are distributed through an elongated plan, while the upper floor is dominated by recreational and storage accommodation rather than conventional bedroom suites. The arrangement provides considerable overall floorspace but does not correspond particularly closely with contemporary expectations for a large family residence of this scale, where greater emphasis would ordinarily be placed upon interconnected kitchen, dining and living spaces, bedroom suites, clearer circulation and stronger relationships with the surrounding landscape.

### External Materials and Detailing

The external treatment incorporates white painted/rendered walling, areas of exposed local-looking rubble stone, dark slate or slate-effect pitched roofs, extensive brown-stained timber windows and doors, timber boarding to gable features and substantial lead or metalwork detailing. The conservatory introduces a contrasting lightweight aluminium-framed glazed structure, while externally mounted renewable-energy equipment, solar collectors and associated technical installations add further layers to the composition. Individually many of these materials are appropriate to a rural setting; collectively, however, they lack a consistent architectural hierarchy and contribute to the visually piecemeal character of the building.

### Architectural Aesthetic

The house has a distinctly period-specific aesthetic, combining elements of 1960s and later twentieth-century domestic architecture with bespoke engineering-led interventions. Its character is neither conventionally traditional nor convincingly contemporary. Large areas of white render, dark roof covering, reddish-brown joinery, exposed stonework and extensive technical additions produce a visually heavy and somewhat dated appearance. The architecture appears to have been driven primarily by functionality, experimentation, energy efficiency and the personal interests of its former owner rather than by the creation of a unified architectural language.

![10__ExistingHouse__Front-04](./02_StatementDocs__Content__Images/10__ExistingHouse__Photos/10__ExistingHouse__Front-04.jpg)

Fig #.# |  Existing Dwelling  -  A Birdseye View Of The Frontage

### Interior Character and Condition

The interior reinforces this impression. Much of the fitted accommodation, particularly the kitchen and utility areas, retains a late twentieth-century palette of cream laminate cabinetry, dark timber trims, brown vinyl flooring, tiled splashbacks and timber wall panelling. Although generally serviceable, the combination now appears visually dated and comparatively dark when considered against contemporary residential standards. The extensive timber trims, deep brown finishes, older fitted furniture and utilitarian lighting create an interior that feels increasingly tired and visually enclosed despite the generous dimensions of several rooms.

### Relationship Between Architecture and Setting

There is a marked contrast between the quality of the wider landscape setting and the architectural quality of the existing dwelling. The property occupies an exceptional mature landscape containing extensive woodland, open lawns, a substantial lake and carefully established planting; however, the existing house does not fully capitalise upon this setting. Its elongated internal planning, relatively conventional window arrangements and piecemeal additions provide only limited architectural engagement with the wider landscape when compared with the opportunities presented by the site.

### Later Additions and Technical Development

The property is notable for the degree to which it has been modified in pursuit of environmental performance and self-sufficiency. The sales particulars confirm that the large conservatory was a later architectural addition and that an additional external wall system was installed around the dwelling in approximately 2009 to improve thermal efficiency. The estate was also progressively equipped with renewable-energy infrastructure, including water-source heating, solar generation and battery storage. These interventions form an important part of the property's history, but they have also contributed to the building's increasingly complex and visually layered appearance. ([OnTheMarket](https://www.onthemarket.com/details/17064229/))

### Overall Design Assessment

The existing dwelling is substantial, unusual and historically interesting for its association with experimental low-carbon and self-sufficient technologies; however, this technical interest is not matched by equivalent architectural coherence. Its low, sprawling form, incremental additions, mixed material palette and strongly period-specific interiors now present as dated and visually tired. The building has clearly evolved around the particular functional and technological interests of its former occupier, resulting in a highly personalised house which is increasingly poorly aligned with contemporary expectations for a dwelling of this scale and setting. In architectural terms, the principal opportunity presented by redevelopment is therefore not simply to increase accommodation, but to replace an ageing and somewhat fragmented composition with a carefully resolved dwelling capable of responding more successfully to the exceptional landscape in which it sits.

## EXISTING SITE INFO

### Site Context and Character

West Beacon Farm is a substantial and established rural residential holding situated within the Charnwood Forest landscape. The existing dwelling occupies a secluded position well within the site and is set within extensive landscaped grounds, mature woodland, open grassland and ancillary land. Development is therefore concentrated within an established estate setting rather than presenting as an isolated dwelling directly addressing the public highway.

### Site Ownership and Adjoining Solar Land

The application site forms part of the historic West Beacon Farm holding, which appears to have originally comprised a larger and more regular agricultural landholding of approximately 50 acres. Available title and planning information indicates that the holding has subsequently been subdivided, with the residential property and associated land now comprising approximately 29 acres. A separate elongated parcel of approximately 20 acres lies immediately adjoining and partially interlocks with the application site. This adjoining land is understood to be in separate ownership and includes the West Beacon solar development, although the photovoltaic installation occupies only part of the wider parcel. The unusual configuration of the current site boundary therefore appears to result from the historic subdivision of the wider West Beacon Farm holding rather than from the natural pattern of the original fields. The proposed development will be contained wholly within the applicant’s landholding, with the adjoining solar land remaining outside the application site.

### Existing Dwelling

The site contains an established detached dwelling understood to date principally from the 1960s, with subsequent alteration and upgrading. The existing accommodation is substantial, comprising approximately 456 m² of residential floorspace based upon the available marketing floor plans, arranged predominantly over ground floor with additional first-floor accommodation. The existing dwelling therefore establishes a significant residential presence within the site.

### Existing Built Development

In addition to the principal dwelling, the site contains a considerable amount of established ancillary built development. This includes garaging, workshops, storage buildings, service buildings and a substantial purpose-built helicopter hangar. Available plans indicate that the wider estate contains approximately 965 m² of gross internal floorspace across the principal buildings shown. The site should therefore be considered in the context of an already extensively developed residential estate rather than as undeveloped countryside.

### Helicopter Hangar and Ancillary Buildings

A substantial helicopter hangar is located separately from the principal dwelling within the wider landscaped grounds. The building extends to approximately 182 m² and historically accommodated helicopter operations associated with the property. Further buildings include a triple garage and storage complex, workshops and a range of ancillary service and storage structures. These buildings form part of the established character and development history of the holding.

### Access and Arrival

Vehicular access is obtained from Deans Lane via a long private drive extending through mature vegetation before reaching the principal residential and ancillary buildings. The dwelling is therefore considerably set back from the public highway and is largely screened from wider public views. Recent planning history also records works associated with access, hardstanding and entrance arrangements within the wider West Beacon Farm holding.

### Landscape Setting

The site is characterised by extensive mature landscaping, including established woodland, individual mature trees, lawns, open grassland, orchard and other managed landscape areas. The combination of mature vegetation and the depth of the site provides a strong degree of visual containment around the existing dwelling and associated development. Any replacement dwelling would be designed in response to this established landscape structure, with retention of significant vegetation and existing screening forming an important part of the design approach.

### Woodland and Trees

Significant woodland belts are present around substantial parts of the property, particularly towards the western and southern areas of the holding. Local environmental records identify areas of deciduous woodland within and around West Beacon Farm, including Priority Habitat woodland. The existing tree structure is therefore recognised as an important component of the site's character and ecological value and would be appropriately considered through arboricultural assessment as the design progresses.

### Lake and Water Features

A substantial established lake is located immediately to the north-west of the principal developed area and forms an important landscape feature within the estate. Additional ponds and water features are also present within the wider holding. Historic records indicate the presence of watercourses associated with West Beacon Farm, including Wood Brook. These existing water features contribute materially to the site's landscape character, ecology and existing renewable-energy infrastructure.

### Ecology

The combination of mature woodland, established trees, grassland, water bodies and existing buildings provides a varied range of habitats within the property. Existing ecological records also identify bat activity within the wider West Beacon Farm area. Ecological considerations will therefore form an early component of the development process, with appropriate survey work undertaken to inform the siting, design and landscape strategy for any replacement dwelling.

### Existing Services and Utilities

The property has an established private infrastructure network appropriate to its rural location. Available information indicates a three-phase electricity supply, private borehole water provision, rainwater harvesting and private foul drainage. The existing dwelling also incorporates renewable and low-carbon technologies, including a water-source heat pump associated with the lake, photovoltaic generation and battery storage. The redevelopment of the property provides an opportunity to rationalise and upgrade this existing infrastructure as part of a comprehensive replacement-dwelling proposal.

### Renewable Energy Infrastructure

West Beacon Farm has a long-standing association with renewable-energy technologies. In addition to renewable systems serving the residential property, a substantial photovoltaic installation has been developed on adjoining land formerly associated with the wider farm holding. The adjoining solar development is physically and functionally distinct from the residential land now forming the application site and will remain outside the proposed development area.

### Former Aviation Infrastructure

The property historically incorporated private aviation facilities associated with the helicopter hangar and landing area. Available property information also records historic fuel-storage infrastructure within the vicinity of the hangar. The location and status of any remaining tanks or associated infrastructure will be established as part of the normal technical due-diligence process where relevant to the proposed works.

### Planning History

West Beacon Farm has an established and varied planning history relating to the dwelling, access arrangements, renewable-energy infrastructure and ancillary development. Of particular relevance is the recent confirmation of the lawful residential use of the principal property, together with the separate planning permissions associated with the adjoining photovoltaic development. The proposed replacement dwelling will therefore be considered against a well-established lawful residential baseline.

### Historic Development of the Site

Historic mapping and local records indicate that West Beacon Farm has evolved considerably over time. Earlier agricultural buildings and farmstead arrangements have been substantially altered or replaced, with the site progressively developing into the present residential estate and associated ancillary uses. The current built form is therefore the product of successive phases of redevelopment rather than a surviving intact historic farmstead.

### Heritage Considerations

West Beacon Farm appears within the local Historic Environment Record, reflecting the historic agricultural use of the site and former farmstead development. Available evidence indicates that much of the earlier farm complex had already been redeveloped by the later twentieth century. The significance of any surviving heritage features will nevertheless be reviewed as part of the design process so that the replacement dwelling can respond appropriately to the established character and history of the site.

### Relationship with the Wider Countryside

Although the property occupies a rural location, the principal dwelling is positioned within a substantial landscaped estate containing extensive existing buildings, hardstanding, access infrastructure and domestic landscape features. The redevelopment proposal will therefore focus upon the established residential core of the site and avoid unnecessary encroachment into the surrounding open countryside.

### Replacement Dwelling Strategy

The emerging proposal is for the demolition of the existing dwelling and construction of a high-quality replacement residence within the established residential holding. The design process will take account of the scale and footprint of the existing dwelling, the wider amount of built development already present on the estate, existing landscape screening, topography, ecology and the relationship with adjoining land. The intention is to establish an appropriate replacement dwelling that responds specifically to the character and opportunities of this unusual site.

### Development Footprint and Visual Containment

The substantial depth of the property and its mature landscape structure provide considerable scope to accommodate redevelopment within an already developed and visually contained part of the estate. The siting of the replacement dwelling will therefore be informed by the existing developed footprint and surrounding vegetation, with the objective of maintaining the secluded character of the site and limiting wider landscape effects.

### Technical and Environmental Assessment

As the design develops, the proposal will be supported by the technical information appropriate to its scale and location. This is expected to include topographical and measured surveys, arboricultural assessment, ecological appraisal, drainage and foul-water considerations and review of the site's existing utility and renewable-energy infrastructure. Any further specialist assessment identified through the pre-application process can then be incorporated proportionately into the subsequent planning submission.
